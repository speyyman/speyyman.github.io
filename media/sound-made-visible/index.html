Sound, made visible

Sand on a steel plate, shaken at one frequency at a time. At most frequencies nothing happens. At a few, the sand runs to the lines that stay still.

Sand on a plate

Take a thin metal plate, hold it at one point, scatter sand on it and make it ring. The sand jumps, slides and settles into lines: a cross, a pair of curves, a ring, a star. Change the note and the lines change. Ernst Chladni published figures like these in 1787, and they still carry his name.

The film above makes them with a computer instead of a violin bow. The plate is a 24 cm square of steel, 1 mm thick, then a disc of the same steel. It is shaken at one point at one frequency at a time, and grains of sand respond to how hard the plate moves under them. Nothing in the program draws a line. The lines are where the sand ends up.

Still lines

A plate does not vibrate the same way at every frequency. At most frequencies a steady shake barely moves it. At a few, its resonances, it moves a great deal, and it moves in a fixed shape called a mode. Some parts go up while others go down, and between them run lines that do not move at all: the nodal lines.

Sand on a moving part is thrown into the air and lands somewhere slightly different. Sand near a nodal line is not thrown, but it still creeps slowly towards the line. Grains wander out of the moving parts and stop on the still ones, and after a few seconds the nodal lines are drawn in sand. Michael Faraday studied this in 1831 and found that it depends on the grain. Sand goes to the nodal lines, but very fine powder gathers where the plate moves most, carried there by currents in the air the plate stirs up. With most of the air pumped out of a jar around a vibrating membrane, his powder went to the still parts too.

The figure below is the same simulation as the film. Drag the frequency, or step from one resonance to the next. Between resonances the plate hardly moves, and the sand keeps the last figure it made.

Where you shake it matters

A mode can only be excited if the plate moves at the point where it is driven. Shake a plate exactly on one of a mode’s nodal lines and that resonance never appears, however long you wait. A plate shaken at its centre can never show the plate’s lowest mode on its own, because its two nodal lines cross at the centre.

So a real set of Chladni figures depends on more than the plate: where it is held, where it is bowed, how stiff the clamp is. The plates here are free at every edge and shaken near one edge. A plate bolted at its centre rings at different frequencies, and these simulations do not include a bolt.

A plate nobody could solve

The square plate with free edges is the very one Chladni used, and it is harder than it looks. Rayleigh wrote in 1894 that the problem of a rectangular plate with free edges “is one of great difficulty, and has for the most part resisted attack”. There is no formula for its modes. In 1909 Walther Ritz used it to introduce a way of solving problems that have no formula: write the shape as a sum of simple functions, and choose the mix that makes the ratio of bending energy to motion energy as small as possible. Leissa’s survey of plate vibration calls it his famous method.

The square here is solved by that method, with polynomials, on the reader’s own computer. Its first six frequency parameters agree with Yoshihiro Narita’s 2022 tables to five significant figures. On this steel plate the lowest resonance is at 56.8 Hz, and there are 38 below 1,600 Hz.

The figures most often shown for this plate come from a shortcut: add two waves of the form cos mπx cos nπy, one turned 90 degrees. The shortcut is old. Rayleigh, in 1894, credits Charles Wheatstone with first using it to explain Chladni’s figures, and adds that its results apply properly to a stretched membrane, not to a plate. Rayleigh calls Wheatstone’s results only very roughly applicable to a plate, and Arthur Leissa’s 1969 survey for NASA prints the formula as an approximation, in its account of M. D. Waller’s measurements. The figures here use the plate, not the shortcut.

The disc

A disc is kinder. Its modes have an exact form, built from Bessel functions, and each one is named by two counts: how many straight nodal lines cross the centre (diameters) and how many rings there are (circles). The lowest has two diameters and no circle; the next has no diameter and one circle, at 0.68 of the radius.

The same lines elsewhere

Nodal lines are not about sand or steel. They belong to anything that holds a standing wave inside an edge.

A drum is the closest relative. In The Theory of Sound, Rayleigh worked out the modes of a circular membrane, Bessel functions again, and reported the experiments of M. Bourget, who sounded paper membranes with organ pipes and sand. Bourget found that the membrane answered only certain notes, that a little above or below them the sand fell into confusion, and that the nodal lines were circles and diameters, as theory requires. That is the behaviour of the figures above, observed more than a century earlier with paper and pipes.

The same mathematics turns up at a much smaller scale. In 1993 Michael Crommie, Christopher Lutz and Donald Eigler built a ring of iron atoms on a copper surface and looked inside with a scanning tunnelling microscope. The electrons trapped in it showed the pattern expected for an electron in a round two-dimensional box: rings of standing waves, the same family of solutions as a drum head.

And instrument makers test plates on their own, as Chladni did. Carleen Hutchins described in 1981 how tests of the vibration of a violin’s top and back plates, before the instrument is assembled, reveal something of what violin makers do by feel.

What this does not show

The sand is a simple statistical model. Grains are thrown a random distance that grows with the plate’s acceleration and stop where the plate barely moves. It leaves out bouncing, friction, the air and grains falling off the edge, so it shows where sand ends up, not how a single grain gets there.

The plate is ideal: thin, uniform, free at every edge and lightly damped, with a damping value I chose rather than measured. The frequencies in hertz depend on handbook values for steel and on the thickness. The frequency parameters, and the shapes of the lines, do not.

You can drive the plate yourself, with more controls, an optional tone, and the disc, in the standalone tool. The code, its tests against the published tables, and the script that renders the film are open.
