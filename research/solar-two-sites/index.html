How much of a solar forecast is skill?

A day-ahead study at two public solar plants: what machine-learning models add over simple baselines, how much of that carries over to another plant, and where it breaks.

Status
Working paper, version 1.0, not peer reviewed
Date
23 October 2026
Author
Saeed Peyman, independent researcher
DOI
10.5281/zenodo.23604923
Licence

Abstract

A solar plant makes power only when the weather allows, so the people who run the grid rely on forecasts, made a day ahead, of how much each plant will produce in every hour of the next day. These forecasts are usually reported as one error number for one plant. This study asks how much of that number is skill, how much depends on the place, and where the skill runs out.

It uses two public plants from NREL’s PVDAQ archive in contrasting climates: an 893 kW fixed array in Arbuckle, California and a 4,738 kW single-axis tracker in Kersey, Colorado. The weather inputs are forecasts as they were issued a day ahead (GFS and ECMWF, archived by Open-Meteo), with the ERA5 reanalysis as hindsight. Every forecaster is scored on the same daytime hours with a monthly rolling origin: 6,009 hours over 549 days at Arbuckle and 3,078 hours over 298 days at Kersey.

Against smart persistence, gradient-boosted trees on GFS forecasts reach a skill of +0.25 at Arbuckle and +0.22 at Kersey. A physical model with two fitted numbers gets most of the way there, and at Kersey the trees add nothing measurable over it. Hindsight weather from the reanalysis adds little. A model trained on the other plant keeps less than half the gain at Arbuckle. Read one by one, many of the largest misses are not weather-forecast errors at all.

The study

Plants
Arbuckle, CA (PVDAQ 2107): 893 kW DC, fixed, 25° tilt. Kersey, CO (PVDAQ 9068): 4,738 kW DC, single-axis tracker.
Weather
Day-ahead GFS (primary) and ECMWF IFS, each value issued 24 hours ahead. ERA5 reanalysis as hindsight.
Scored
Arbuckle 2024-07-01 to 2025-12-31, Kersey 2024-07-01 to 2025-04-29. Daytime hours only.
Baselines
Persistence (tomorrow repeats today), smart persistence (today’s cloudiness applied to tomorrow’s clear-sky output), and a pvlib physical model from the plant metadata.
Models
Calibrated physical model, LightGBM point and quantile trees, and a model trained on the other plant.
Tested on
Each month, by models trained only on earlier months. Intervals from a block bootstrap of whole weeks.

Results

Errors are root-mean-square errors as fractions of each plant’s AC rating, so 0.2 means a typical miss of roughly a fifth of the plant’s full output. Skill is the share of the baseline’s error a forecaster removes: zero is no better than the baseline, one is perfect.

Baseline error
Smart persistence, normalised RMSE: 0.194 at Arbuckle, 0.232 at Kersey.
Physical
From metadata alone: +0.08 and +0.11. With a fitted scale and offset: +0.19 and +0.21.
Boosted trees
+0.25 (95% interval +0.20 to +0.29) at Arbuckle, +0.22 (+0.14 to +0.29) at Kersey.
Hindsight
Trees given the weather that happened: +0.26 and +0.29.
Other plant
Trees trained at the other site: +0.10 and +0.16.
Intervals
Nominal 80% bands hold 59% and 65% of hours. Too narrow at both.

What the learning adds

Most of the skill comes from physics and two fitted numbers. Against the calibrated physical model, the trees are measurably better at Arbuckle and not measurably different at Kersey.

Better weather would help Kersey somewhat and Arbuckle hardly at all. Giving the trees the reanalysis, the weather that actually happened, lifts skill from +0.25 to +0.26 at Arbuckle and from +0.22 to +0.29 at Kersey. Most of what is left is in how weather becomes power at each plant, in local conditions no grid model resolves, and in the plants themselves.

What does not travel is the relation between one site’s forecasts and one plant’s behaviour. Trained at the other plant, the trees keep less than half their gain at Arbuckle and about three quarters at Kersey. That part of the result is about one place.

Where it is easy

Arbuckle’s summers are easy for everything: from June to August the baseline’s error is 0.121 and the trees reach only +0.13. Winter and spring are where forecasting pays, at +0.27 and +0.28. Kersey’s record holds one of each season, two of them partial; its strongest is spring (+0.35, March and April 2025 only) and its weakest autumn (+0.05).

Where it failed

The worst tenth of days carries a quarter of all absolute error at both plants. Sorted by fixed rules, many of the biggest misses are not errors of the weather forecast. Some are the plant: zero output on two clear days, an inverter off for a week. Some are unclear: a summer of clear, dry days at reduced output, which could be equipment, soiling or smoke. Some are weather that no source saw, including still winter days that look like fog. Days on which the forecast was wrong and the reanalysis right are a minority. Leaving out the days a forecast-free rule flags as plant anomalies raises the trees’ skill to +0.29 at Arbuckle and +0.25 at Kersey.

Limitations

Two plants, neither of them a cloudy, humid site: these were the public plants I found with data in the years the forecast archive covers. Kersey has 16 months, ten of them scored. The first test month has about five and a half months of training history. Weather is hourly at a single grid point. Plant faults are inferred from power, power factor and weather; without maintenance logs the failure categories are descriptions, not diagnoses. Hyperparameters were fixed in advance and not tuned.

This is a new, independent study. It was prompted by my earlier team capstone on solar yield, and it shares no code, data or analysis with it.

Read and reuse

The whole study reruns from one command, from the raw downloads to every number in the paper. All data are public, and the sources and licences are listed in the repository.
