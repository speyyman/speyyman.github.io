Solar yield forecast

Predicting solar output from weather data. Messy inputs, careful models.

Year
2022 – 2023
Role
Team capstone, MIT ReACT
Status
Capstone complete
Built with
Python, time-series weather data, forecasting models

Overview

A photovoltaic yield forecasting tool, built as the team capstone for the MIT ReACT data science certificate. It predicts how much electricity solar panels will produce from ordinary weather data, then uses that to model what the output means for the electrical grid in Mafraq City, Jordan.

The interesting part turned out to be less the model than the data. Real readings have gaps, drift, and sensors that quietly disagree with each other.

The knot

A forecast is only as good as its inputs, and ours were time series from different sources, on different clocks, with holes in them. Grid planners need a number they can trust, so most of the early effort went into deciding which values could be.

My role

This was a team capstone, worked on with fellow students in the cohort. I took on much of the data preparation and the forecasting models, and we presented the grid-impact analysis together.

Being part of a selective global cohort meant the team came from very different backgrounds, which showed up in how many different ways we found to check each other’s work.

How it went

We cleaned before we modelled. Sources were aligned to one clock, suspect readings were flagged, and every value that had to be filled in was recorded, so we always knew how much of a result rested on guesswork.

A simple physical estimate set the baseline, so each model had to beat something honest rather than something easy.

Models were scored on whole stretches of time they had never seen, not on shuffled rows, because shuffling quietly leaks the future into the past and flatters every result.

Where it landed

The better models beat the baseline most clearly on partly cloudy days, which is exactly where the simple estimate struggles.

The write-up records where each model fails as well as where it works, and that honesty made the grid-impact analysis easier to defend.

Decisions I’d defend

Scoring on unseen stretches of time, not shuffled rows, was the choice that kept the results honest. Shuffling flatters every model, and we would rather know the real number.

Keeping a record of every filled-in value let us say how much of any result depended on guesswork, which is the first question a careful reader asks.
